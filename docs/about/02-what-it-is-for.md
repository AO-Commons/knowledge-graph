The field is young, fast, and scattered across venues that do not cite each
other. The expensive thing is not finding papers. It is finding out whether
anyone has already shown the thing you are about to assume — and whether
anyone disagrees.

| The question | What you get | Why not just search |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Has anyone already shown this? | the statements that say it, each with the sentence it came from and who claimed it | search returns papers; you still read twenty abstracts to find the one sentence |
| Who disagrees, and about what? | statements asserted to contradict one another, each with the reason somebody gave | a citation graph tells you what cites what, never whether it agrees |
| Who has found this, working that way? | a finding's subject joined to a method's technique | no index separates what a paper is about from how it was done |
| What should I know before I trust this finding? | the background it rests on and the limitations its own authors stated | limitations live in discussion sections and are the first thing a reader loses |
| What does the field currently hold on X? | a citable release, fixed and checksummed, that will say the same thing next year | a literature review is stale on publication and cannot be queried |

**For a researcher**, it is a way to enter a subject and come out with the
claims and the disagreements rather than a reading list. **For a
practitioner**, it is a way to check a design decision against what has
actually been demonstrated. **For an agent**, it is a queryable substrate:
structured claims with provenance, which is what a model needs to answer
carefully rather than plausibly.

**What makes it trustworthy is the same thing that makes it slow.** Machines
find, resolve, admit and tag. Every statement here has been checked
mechanically — its quote was found verbatim in the source, its tags resolve,
and it is about the field rather than about the paper's own instrument — which
is why {{machineChecked}} of {{statements}} say machine-checked. That is a
real signal and it is not review.

What no machine does is decide whether the statement says what its source
says. A person does that, and the judgment is recorded against their name in a
pull request anyone can read. Today that is {{reviewed}} of {{statements}} —
the honest state of the library, and the reason the Review tab exists.
