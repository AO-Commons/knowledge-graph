---
id: why
title: Statements, not papers
kicker: the design choice
order: 3
---

A paper is a container. The thing worth linking is what it **asserts** — and
two papers are almost never about the same thing, while two sentences often
are.

That choice decides everything downstream. Tags go on statements rather than
on papers, which lets them be far more specific: a tag naming one mechanism
puts two statements side by side that are genuinely worth reading together,
where a tag naming a whole subfield would put hundreds of unrelated ones
there. Relations run between statements, so "who disagrees with this" has an
answer more precise than "this paper, somewhere". And a paper's categories are
computed from the tags on its statements rather than assigned by hand, which
is why a paper lands in several at once, honestly.

The corpus is deliberately narrow. Admitting everything cited by everything
held measures out at roughly 4,900 records, 93% of them cited exactly once.
{{records}} curated records are worth more than 8,500 indiscriminate ones.
